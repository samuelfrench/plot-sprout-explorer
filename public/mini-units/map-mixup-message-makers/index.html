Teacher mini-unit

Map Mix-Up Message Makers

Children turn bakery maps, errand slips, and lunchbox harbor orders into clear directions, short dialogue, and a practical final message.

Back to mini-units
Ages 7-9
3 lessons, 30 minutes each
Homeschool parents, co-op leaders, and grade 2-3 teachers who want a short map, direction, and dialogue writing unit.

Objectives

  • Use a simple map to plan a fictional route with two or three stops.
  • Write directions that include order words such as first, next, and last.
  • Create a short polite dialogue that clarifies a mixed-up message.

Materials

  • Blank paper for maps
  • Pencils and colored pencils
  • Sticky notes or small paper slips
  • Classroom board or parent whiteboard
  • Optional toy counters for route stops
  • Timer

Lesson flow

30 minutes

Bakery Map Draft

Teacher move: Read the Button Bakery premise and draw a four-stop map with a bakery, one helper shop, one turn, and one finish spot.

Student task: Students draw their own simple map and place two or three stops that a helper can visit in order.

Output: Labeled route map with a clear start and finish.

30 minutes

Errand Office Directions

Teacher move: Use Acorn Avenue to model turning a map into three direction sentences that start with first, next, and last.

Student task: Students write three ordered direction sentences for their map and trade with a partner or adult for a clarity check.

Output: Three-sentence direction card.

30 minutes

Harbor Order Dialogue

Teacher move: Introduce Spoon Ferry and Sticker Station as examples of friendly clarification, then model a two-speaker dialogue that asks one useful question.

Student task: Students add a short dialogue to their direction card so one character can clarify a mixed-up order or message.

Output: Map, direction card, and two-speaker clarification dialogue.

Teacher notes

  • Keep maps small: four locations are enough for this age band.
  • The lesson works with blank paper; printed worksheets are optional, not required.
  • Do not require children to use real addresses, real classmates, or personal family details.
  • If partner work is distracting, use adult or teacher checks instead of peer checks.
  • For handwriting fatigue, let students write the direction card and dictate the dialogue.

Quest worlds used

  • Button Bakery Map Mix-Up: A bakery the size of a lunchbox sells button-shaped rolls, and every customer gets a crumb map to the next helpful errand.
  • Acorn Avenue Errand Office: A desk inside a hollow acorn sorts tiny neighborhood errands: return a borrowed thimble, update the snack map, and deliver cheerful notes before the lunch bell.
  • Spoon Ferry Lunchbox Harbor: At Lunchbox Harbor, spoon ferries carry menu cards, snack orders, and polite dockside dialogue between tiny cracker cafes.
  • Sticker Station Mail Cart: A rolling greeting cart visits a sticker shop, a pencil repair nook, and a tiny lost-and-found counter where every short note needs the right friendly mood.

Homeschool and classroom use

Homeschool adaptation: Spread the lessons across a week and use household objects as route markers. A parent can walk the route with the child before the child writes the three direction sentences.

Classroom management: Prepare small paper slips before the lesson and limit movement by having students point to map stops instead of walking routes around the room.

Assessment: Look for a start and finish on the map, three ordered direction sentences, and a dialogue line that asks or answers one clear question.
