Teacher mini-unit

Craft Academy Procedure Polish

Students revise a how-to page by testing whether a reader can follow each step, then tighten sequence words, remove filler exposition, and add useful cautions.

Back to mini-units
Ages 10-11
50 minutes
Upper elementary homeschool groups or classrooms practicing procedural writing, exposition control, and feedback that improves usability.

Objectives

  • Sequence a procedure so each step can be followed without extra explanation.
  • Replace vague verbs and filler exposition with precise action language.
  • Use feedback to add one helpful margin note, caution, or materials clarification.

Materials

  • Drafted how-to page or teacher-provided procedure sample
  • Procedure test cards
  • Margin-note strips
  • Pencils
  • Checklist for precise verbs, sequence words, and materials

Lesson flow

15 minutes

Run the Reader Test

Teacher move: Demonstrate following a procedure exactly as written, pausing when a missing material, unclear verb, or skipped sequence makes the step hard to complete.

Student task: Students trade procedures with a partner or adult and mark any place where the reader has to guess.

Output: A procedure draft with reader-test marks.

20 minutes

Polish the Steps

Teacher move: Model changing vague directions into precise steps with clear sequence words, exact materials, and only the background information the reader needs.

Student task: Students revise at least three steps by tightening verbs, adding sequence cues, or moving extra explanation into a short note.

Output: A clearer procedure with revised steps.

15 minutes

Add the Useful Margin Note

Teacher move: Show examples of margin notes that help the reader avoid confusion without taking over the whole page.

Student task: Students add one margin note, caution, or materials clarification based on reader feedback.

Output: A polished procedure page with one reader-tested support note.

Teacher notes

  • Choose low-stakes procedures such as folding a paper booklet, sorting an appendix card set, or building a paper compass marker.
  • Do not let students redesign the whole activity; the target is clearer writing, not a new project.
  • When feedback feels too broad, ask the reader to point to the exact step where guessing started.
  • Encourage precise verbs such as fold, align, label, compare, circle, and check.
  • Keep safety notes practical and classroom-based, such as asking an adult before using messy supplies.

Quest worlds used

  • Compass Craft Academy: At a school for story cartographers, students learn to plan routes before writing the journey.
  • Almost Invention Workshop: Inventors bring almost-working ideas to a workshop where revision is treated like testing, adjusting, and trying again.
  • Appendix Archive Lab: A hidden archive stores maps, notes, glossaries, timelines, and side details that help writers build believable worlds.
  • Margin Note Market: At a bustling market for feedback, every stall trades clear margin notes that help writers make the next draft stronger.

Homeschool and classroom use

Homeschool adaptation: Have the child hand the procedure to an adult or sibling who silently follows the steps; the child records every pause as a revision target before rewriting.

Classroom management: Pair students by draft length, keep materials at a central supply point, and require quiet reader tests before discussion so feedback is based on the written directions.

Assessment: Review the final procedure for clear sequence, precise verbs, complete materials, and one margin note that responds to real reader feedback.
