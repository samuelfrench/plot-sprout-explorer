Adult guide

Story signal coaching

Before the session

  • Choose four to six paper signal cards for the table; place blank paper and pencils within reach.
  • Say that every porch-light mark is pretend and belongs to a made-up story setting.
  • Pick one writing goal: start, problem, helper, mood, or ending.
  • Give each writer a choice of two cards, then let the adult read the prompt aloud.
  • Keep the first draft short: one sentence, three sentences, or a tiny scene.

Paper signal setup

  • Print cards on plain paper or cardstock and cut along the card edges.
  • Sort cards into color, pattern, message, mood, object, and ending piles.
  • Set a pencil cup, blank slips, and a scrap-paper spot in the center.
  • Use a paper clip or folder to keep unused cards together between sessions.
  • Mark a sample card as the adult example, then invite writers to make their own signal marks.

Story signal coaching

  • Ask what the signal tells the character to notice next.
  • Turn one-word answers into story sentences by adding who, where, and why.
  • When a writer gets stuck, offer two gentle choices instead of supplying the whole idea.
  • Point to the card color or shape before asking for more detail.
  • Let funny, odd, or quiet ideas stay; the goal is a usable story seed.

Privacy and safety notes

  • Use invented labels, role words, or blank spaces for every person on the story signal cards.
  • Use broad place words instead of private details or named locations.
  • Keep every story signal card offline with the family adult, tutor, or table host.
  • Sharing stays optional and limited to one invented word, sketch, or line.
  • Check every take-home slip for private details before it leaves the adult-led table.

Family handoff

  • Send only the paper slip and a short note about the story skill practiced.
  • Tell family adults the signals are fiction cues for paper stories.
  • Invite a writer to read one favorite sentence aloud during a calm table moment.
  • Suggest that families add one extra line, one title, or one ending choice.
  • Return slips in a folder or envelope for the next adult-led session.

Reset

  • Collect used cards and blank slips.
  • Recycle torn scraps and save clear drafts in the folder.
  • Shuffle each card pile so the next group sees a fresh mix.
  • Note which cue type helped most, then start the next session with that type.

Use one paper signal card at a time. Keep every choice broad, invented, and screen-free.

Signal routines

Choose the paper signal rhythm

Color Glow Quickwrite

Warm-up sentences for ages 6-8.

  1. Adult fans three color cue cards face up.
  2. Writer chooses one color and says what feeling it gives the scene.
  3. Everyone writes one sentence that uses the color signal.
  4. Adult invites one volunteer sentence, then moves to a new card.

Pattern Pass

Small groups practicing repeated details.

  1. Adult gives one pattern card to the first writer.
  2. Writer adds a sentence with a repeated mark or shape.
  3. Pass the card to the next writer at the table.
  4. Adult ends after each writer has added one line.

Message Flip

Reluctant writers who need a clear next move.

  1. Adult folds a blank slip and places it under a message cue card.
  2. Writer writes a short fictional signal message on the slip.
  3. Adult flips a question cue card beside it.
  4. Writer answers as the character and turns the answer into a scene line.

Mood Dimmer

Revising a sentence so it carries a stronger feeling.

  1. Adult chooses one plain sentence from a draft.
  2. Writer places a mood cue above the sentence.
  3. Writer rewrites the sentence to match that mood.
  4. Adult circles one stronger verb or describing word.

Object Signal Swap

Adding concrete details to a short scene.

  1. Adult places an object cue beside a porch-light card.
  2. Writer names what the character carries or finds.
  3. Writer writes one reason the object matters.
  4. Swap the object cue and revise one noun or action.

Ending Glow

Finishing short drafts that stop too soon.

  1. Adult chooses an ending cue and reads it aloud.
  2. Writer picks a quiet, funny, or surprising ending path.
  3. Writer writes the last two lines on paper.
  4. Adult asks what changed from the first line to the last.
