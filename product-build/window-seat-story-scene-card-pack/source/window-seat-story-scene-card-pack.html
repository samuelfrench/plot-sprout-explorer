Adult guide

Scene story coaching

Before the session

  • Print the scene cards, take-home scene slips, and adult guide before writers arrive.
  • Set the adult-led rule: every idea stays on paper with pencils, sketches, and short story words.
  • Explain that window-seat means a pretend framed view or imagined window view only.
  • Choose one scene routine before the session so each writer has one clear paper path.
  • Keep the session offline, calm, and focused on one small scene card that can start later drafting.

Window-seat setup

  • Place blank scene cards, pencils, broad view-word cards, and slips where the adult can see every page.
  • Set six labeled piles: setting scene, character scene, choice scene, sequence scene, sensory scene, and revision scene.
  • Use broad view words only, such as sky, tree, path, hill, garden, porch, sign, bench, or curtain.
  • Leave room on each card for a title, one paper window frame, three scene notes, and one story sentence.
  • Keep extra scene cards in an adult folder so the writing area stays paper-only and easy to reset.

Scene story coaching

  • Ask for one pretend view first, then turn it into a made-up setting with one mood word.
  • Ask for one invented character role who notices, carries, waits, sorts, or chooses something in the scene.
  • Ask for one pretend object inside the framed view and give it a story job.
  • Ask for first, next, and finally lines when the writer needs a clean scene sequence.
  • Ask the writer to improve one sentence by adding a clearer look, sound, pattern, label, or texture word.

Privacy and safety notes

  • Use invented names, role words, or blank labels for every person on the scene cards.
  • Use broad place words instead of private details, named locations, schedules, or personal routines.
  • Keep all window-seat writing fictional, adult-led, offline, and paper-only.
  • The adult keeps finished pages in the family folder, tutor folder, co-op folder, or classroom folder.
  • Check every take-home scene slip for private details before it leaves the adult-led table.

Family handoff

  • Send one finished scene card and one blank take-home scene slip with each writer.
  • Tell family adults that five to nine quiet minutes is enough for one extra scene prompt.
  • Invite the family adult to ask for one pretend view, one invented character, and one pretend object.
  • Ask adults to praise one clear word, sketch, or sentence before suggesting one small revision.
  • Suggest keeping scene cards together so the writer can reuse the strongest framed-view clue in a later printable story.

Reset

  • Collect unused scene cards, take-home scene slips, pencils, and broad view-word cards.
  • Check finished scene cards for private details before they go with the family adult.
  • Recycle scrap pages that only hold warm-up marks.
  • Restock the adult folder with fresh blank scene cards for the next offline session.

Use one paper card at a time. Keep every choice broad, invented, and screen-free.

Scene routines

Choose the paper scene rhythm

Setting Scene

Writers who need one framed place before inventing action.

  1. Adult points to one broad view word on the paper scene card.
  2. Writer draws a simple pretend window frame around the imagined view.
  3. Writer adds one mood word and one look detail to the setting scene.
  4. Writer writes one sentence showing where the story begins.

Character Scene

Kids who like inventing roles, questions, and small choices.

  1. Adult asks the writer to choose an invented role, such as finder, watcher, sorter, keeper, or helper.
  2. Writer gives the character one question about the imagined view.
  3. Writer sketches one clue the character notices in the framed scene.
  4. Writer writes one sentence about what the character does first.

Choice Scene

Families turning a quiet view into a clear story decision.

  1. Adult asks the writer to mark two calm choices on the scene card.
  2. Writer chooses one invented character who must pick between the choices.
  3. Writer adds one reason the chosen path fits the scene.
  4. Writer writes one sentence that shows the choice without naming a private place.

Sequence Scene

Writers who need a tiny beginning, middle, and ending path.

  1. Adult points to the first, next, and finally lines on the scene card.
  2. Writer chooses one pretend view and one invented character.
  3. Writer fills the three lines with calm story steps.
  4. Writer circles the step that should become the first full paragraph.

Sensory Scene

Quiet writers who notice color, sound, shape, pattern, or texture words.

  1. Adult asks the writer to choose one gentle sensory word from the paper prompt list.
  2. Writer pairs that word with a pretend view, object, or invented character.
  3. Writer sketches the detail inside the paper window frame.
  4. Writer writes one sentence where the sensory detail becomes a story clue.

Revision Scene

Writers strengthening one scene sentence from an earlier card or draft.

  1. Adult places the earlier sentence beside a blank revision scene card.
  2. Writer chooses one clearer look, sound, label, pattern, or texture word.
  3. Writer copies the sentence and adds the clearer detail.
  4. Writer underlines the word or phrase that improved the sentence most.
