Adult guide

Opening coaching

Before the session

  • Print the opening cards and choose a small stack that matches the writers' stamina for one sitting.
  • Set out pencils, blank paper, and a pencil cup as the shared idea holder.
  • Tell writers every opening must be fictional, invented, and broad enough that it does not reveal narrow real-world facts.
  • Preview the cards and remove any prompt that feels too close to an actual person or specific real-world detail.
  • Model one simple invented opening first: The green pencil leaned out of the cup and whispered, ____________________.

Pencil cup opening setup

  • Place five pencil colors in the cup and assign each one an opening job: first line, arrival, doorway, object, or question.
  • Let the adult draw the pencil so the activity stays guided and low-pressure.
  • Keep a small scrap pile beside the cup for false starts; writers may cross out and begin again without keeping every attempt.
  • Use invented labels on nearby slips, such as Cloud Hall, Button Market, Moon Desk, or Acorn Station.
  • Ask the writer to finish one opening stem before adding plot: Today the pencil cup held ____________________.

Story opening coaching

  • Start with motion when a writer is stuck: The pencil rolled, pointed, tapped, slipped, or turned toward ____________________.
  • Ask for one clear noun before any explanation: Who or what appears first in the opening? ____________________.
  • Keep the first paragraph small: one sentence for the surprise, one sentence for the place, and one sentence for the first move.
  • When an opening sounds like a summary, ask the writer to move closer to the first moment: The first thing I noticed was ____________________.
  • For gentle revision, circle one word that can become more specific and replace it with an invented detail: ____________________.

Privacy and safety notes

  • Use broad invented labels instead of narrow real-world facts.
  • Keep sharing limited to adult-led paper conversation.
  • Keep the activity paper-only and skip media capture.
  • Keep all characters fictional and avoid details that point to an actual writer.
  • Invite writers to skip any line they do not want to read aloud.

Family handoff

  • Send along only the chosen slip and a blank page, not a log of the writer's full writing history.
  • Invite family adults to ask about the made-up beginning rather than correcting spelling first.
  • Suggest one adult-led line: My favorite invented detail was ____________________.
  • Remind family adults to keep narrow real-world facts and private routines off the story page.
  • Keep the handoff paper-only and adult-led.

Reset

  • Return unused cards to the pack and recycle crossed-out scraps that contain narrow real-world facts.
  • Put the pencil colors back in the cup so the next session starts with the same simple choices.
  • Save only fictional examples that are useful for modeling future openings.
  • Check that take-home slips are blank except for invented story material.

Use one pencil cup opening card at a time. Keep every choice broad, invented, and screen-free.

Opening routines

Choose the pencil cup opening rhythm

First Line Pencil Pull

Writers who need help getting the very first sentence onto the page.

  1. The adult draws one pencil and names its job: first line.
  2. The writer chooses a fictional sound, color, or movement from the pencil cup.
  3. The writer completes this line: The first sign that something was different was ____________________.
  4. The adult asks the writer to add one more sentence showing who noticed it.

Cup Character Arrival

Starting with a character before the setting is fully known.

  1. Choose one pencil and pretend it belongs to an invented character.
  2. Ask what the character carries, wants, or misunderstands.
  3. The writer completes this arrival line: Into the story came ____________________.
  4. The adult prompts one first action, not a full backstory.

Doorway Place Opening

Writers who like places, maps, rooms, and entrances.

  1. Place a folded blank slip beside the pencil cup and call it a doorway.
  2. Name an invented place that could fit behind it.
  3. The writer completes this opening: Behind the pencil cup doorway was ____________________.
  4. The adult asks for one detail the character sees before stepping in.

Object Invitation Start

Turning ordinary desk objects into story starters.

  1. Pick one paper-only object near the cup, such as an eraser, paperclip, ruler, or index card.
  2. Ask what the object seems to invite the character to do.
  3. The writer completes this line: The object on the desk seemed to be inviting ____________________.
  4. The adult asks the writer to write the first yes, no, or maybe response.

Opening Question Card

Writers who start faster when there is a mystery but no scary content.

  1. The adult chooses a card and turns its prompt into a gentle question.
  2. The writer answers with an invented place, object, or character.
  3. The writer completes this question opening: Why was ____________________ waiting in the pencil cup?
  4. The adult reminds the writer not to solve the whole question yet.

First Move Revision

Improving a plain beginning without rewriting the whole page.

  1. The writer underlines the first sentence of a draft opening.
  2. The adult asks which word can become more vivid, specific, or surprising.
  3. The writer rewrites only that sentence using this stem: This time, the opening begins with ____________________.
  4. The adult has the writer compare both versions and choose the one that starts the story faster.
