Adult guide

Desk-clue story coaching

Before the session

  • Print the desk strips, take-home strips, and adult guide before writers arrive.
  • Set the adult-led rule: every choice stays on paper with pencils, sketches, and short story words.
  • Choose one strip routine before the session so each writer has one clear path.
  • Say that a desk strip can hold a setting, character, object, sequence, revision, or sensory clue for a made-up story.
  • Keep the session offline, calm, and focused on one small strip that can start later drafting.

Strip setup

  • Place blank desk strips, pencils, and take-home strips where the adult can see every page.
  • Set six labeled piles: setting strip, character strip, object strip, sequence strip, revision strip, and sensory strip.
  • Use broad desk words only, such as drawer, lamp, pencil cup, notebook, paper tray, shelf, or corner.
  • Leave room on each strip for a title, one small sketch, three short lines, and one story sentence.
  • Keep extra strips in an adult folder so the writing area stays paper-only and easy to reset.

Desk-clue story coaching

  • Ask for one broad desk place first, then turn it into a made-up setting with one mood word.
  • Ask for one invented character role who is looking for, carrying, sorting, fixing, or noticing something.
  • Ask for one pretend desk object and give it a story job such as guiding, hiding, reminding, or sorting.
  • Ask for first, next, and finally lines when the writer needs a clean beginning path.
  • Ask the writer to improve one sentence by adding a clearer look, sound, shape, pattern, or texture word.

Privacy and safety notes

  • Use invented names, role words, or blank labels for every person on the strips.
  • Use broad place words instead of named locations, route details, room numbers, or private routines.
  • Keep all desk-strip writing fictional, adult-led, offline, and paper-only.
  • Keep finished strips with the family adult, tutor, co-op adult, or classroom adult.
  • Check every take-home strip for identifying details before it leaves the adult-led table.

Family handoff

  • Send one finished desk strip and one blank take-home desk strip with each writer.
  • Tell family adults that five to nine quiet minutes is enough for one extra strip prompt.
  • Invite the family adult to ask for one desk place, one invented character, and one pretend object.
  • Ask adults to praise one clear word, sketch, or sentence before suggesting one small revision.
  • Suggest keeping strips together so the writer can reuse the strongest clue in a later printable story.

Reset

  • Collect unused strips, take-home strips, pencils, and broad desk-word cards.
  • Check finished strips for private details before they go home.
  • Recycle scrap pages that only hold warm-up marks.
  • Restock the adult folder with fresh blank desk strips for the next offline session.

Use one paper strip at a time. Keep every choice broad, invented, and screen-free.

Strip formats

Choose the paper strip rhythm

Setting Strip

Writers who need one small place before inventing action.

  1. Adult points to one broad desk word on the paper strip.
  2. Writer chooses a made-up setting name without using a real location.
  3. Writer adds one mood word and one look detail to the strip.
  4. Writer writes one sentence showing where the story begins.

Character Strip

Kids who like inventing roles, questions, and small choices.

  1. Adult asks the writer to choose an invented desk-world role, such as sorter, finder, keeper, or messenger.
  2. Writer gives the character one question or choice.
  3. Writer sketches one clue about what the character notices.
  4. Writer writes one sentence about what the character does first.

Object Strip

Families turning ordinary desk ideas into useful story objects.

  1. Adult asks the writer to invent one pretend desk object or choose one from a printed idea list.
  2. Writer gives the object one story job, such as guiding, hiding, reminding, or sorting.
  3. Writer adds one detail about how the object looks, feels, or sounds.
  4. Writer writes one sentence showing why the object matters.

Sequence Strip

Writers who need a tiny beginning, middle, and ending path.

  1. Adult points to the first, next, and finally lines on the strip.
  2. Writer chooses one desk place and one invented character.
  3. Writer fills the three lines with calm story steps.
  4. Writer circles the step that should become the first full paragraph.

Revision Strip

Writers strengthening one sentence from an earlier strip or draft.

  1. Adult places the earlier sentence beside a blank revision strip.
  2. Writer chooses one clearer look, sound, shape, pattern, or texture word.
  3. Writer copies the sentence and adds the clearer detail.
  4. Writer underlines the word or phrase that improved the sentence most.

Sensory Strip

Quiet writers who notice color, sound, shape, pattern, or texture words.

  1. Adult asks the writer to choose one gentle sensory word from the paper prompt list.
  2. Writer pairs that word with a desk place, object, or invented character.
  3. Writer sketches the detail in the strip corner.
  4. Writer writes one sentence where the sensory detail becomes a story clue.
